
**1. Metallic Bonding & Giant Metallic Structure**

- **Definition:** The electrostatic attraction between a regular lattice of positive metal ions (cations) and a "sea" of mobile, delocalised valence electrons12.
- **Structure:** Giant metallic lattice of closely packed metal cations surrounded by a three-dimensional sea of delocalised electrons12.
- **Factors Affecting Metallic Bond Strength:**
    1. **Cationic Charge:** Higher charge (e.g., $\text{Al}^{3+} > \text{Mg}^{2+} > \text{Na}^+$) contributes more delocalised electrons per atom to the sea, strengthening the electrostatic attraction.
    2. **Cationic Radius:** Smaller cations allow delocalised electrons to get closer to the positive nuclei, increasing bond strength.

**Physical Properties Explained:**

- **Electrical Conductivity:** Good conductors in **both solid and molten states**3. Delocalised valence electrons are highly mobile and act as charge carriers to carry current when a potential difference is applied34.
- **Thermal Conductivity:** Good conductors5. Heated mobile electrons gain kinetic energy, move faster, and collide with neighbouring particles to transfer heat rapidly. Close packing of cations also allows efficient energy transfer via lattice vibrations6.
- **Malleability & Ductility:** Metals can be hammered into sheets (malleable) or pulled into wires (ductile)78. When a force is applied, layers of orderly packed metal ions slip over one another without shattering78. The delocalised electron sea moves with the layers, holding the displaced cations in their new positions7.
- **Melting & Boiling Points:** Generally high5. Large amounts of thermal energy are required to overcome the strong electrostatic attraction between metal cations and the delocalised sea of electrons7.

**Alloys (Mixtures of Metals)**

- **Definition:** A physical mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (e.g., Brass = Copper + Zinc; Solder = Tin + Lead)1more_horiz.
- **Key Relationship (Disrupted Lattice):** Pure metals are soft because identical-sized cations slide past each other easily811.
- In an alloy, alloying atoms have **different sizes**, which **disrupts the regular, orderly arrangement of rows** of metal cations911. This makes it significantly **more difficult for the layers to slide** past one another, making alloys **much harder and stronger** than pure metals911.
- **Melting Point of Alloys:** Because alloys are mixtures, they do not have a fixed melting point but rather a **melting range**, which is typically **lower** than the melting points of its constituent elements (e.g., Solder)10.

**2. Ionic Bonding & Giant Ionic Lattice**

- **Definition:** The electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions (cations and anions) formed through electron transfer1213.
- **Structure:** A **giant ionic lattice structure** containing a regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of alternating alternating cations and anions12more_horiz.
- **Factors Affecting Ionic Bond Strength (Lattice Energy):** $$\text{Electrostatic Attraction} \propto \frac{Q^+ \times Q^-}{r^+ + r^-}$$
    1. **Ionic Charge (**$Q$**):** Higher charges produce much stronger electrostatic attractions (e.g., $\text{MgO}$ with $+2/-2$ charges has a much higher melting point than $\text{NaCl}$ with $+1/-1$ charges)1415.
    2. **Ionic Radius (**$r$**):** Smaller ionic sizes allow the oppositely charged ions to pack closer together, increasing attraction14.

**Physical Properties Explained:**

- **Melting & Boiling Points:** Extremely high1216. Large amounts of energy are required to overcome the strong electrostatic forces holding oppositely charged ions together15.
- **Electrical Conductivity:**
    - **Solid State:** Non-conductor16. Ions are locked in fixed positions within the lattice and are **immobile**, meaning they cannot act as charge carriers1617.
    - **Molten & Aqueous States:** Good conductor16. When melted or dissolved, the giant lattice breaks down, releasing **mobile ions** that can migrate toward electrodes to carry current1617.
- **Brittleness:** Hard but highly **brittle**18. When an external shearing force is applied, layers of ions slide across one another, placing **ions of like charges adjacent to each other**1819. The resulting strong electrostatic repulsion shatters the lattice1819.
- **Solubility:** Generally soluble in polar solvents (water) but insoluble in non-polar organic solvents (e.g., $\text{CCl}_4$)17.
    - _Water Solubility:_ Polar water molecules attract ions at the lattice surface, releasing hydration energy that weakens and overcomes the ionic bonds17.
    - _Organic Insolubility:_ Non-polar solvent molecules lack significant electrostatic attraction to the ions and cannot disrupt the strong lattice structure17.

**3. Covalent Bonding & Simple Molecular Substances**

- **Definition:** The electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of bonding electrons and the positively charged nuclei of the bonded non-metal atoms2021.
- **Structure:** **Simple molecular structure** consists of discrete molecules held together by weak intermolecular forces20more_horiz.
- **Intramolecular vs. Intermolecular Forces (Crucial Exam Distinction):**
    - **Intramolecular:** Very **strong covalent bonds** within the molecule itself2425. These are _never_ broken during physical phase transitions (melting or boiling)2627.
    - **Intermolecular:** **Weak forces of attraction** between discrete molecules2425. Only these weak forces are overcome during melting and boiling2227.
- **Bond Energy:** The amount of energy required to break one mole of a specific covalent bond in the gaseous state28.
    - _Relationship:_ Triple bonds share more electrons, creating greater electrostatic attraction to the nuclei, which results in shorter bond lengths and higher bond energies2028.
    - _Examples to Memorize:_
        - Single bond: $\text{F–F}$ ($154\text{ kJ/mol}$)2829
        - Double bond: $\text{O=O}$ ($495\text{ kJ/mol}$)2829
        - Triple bond: $\text{N}\equiv\text{N}$ ($941\text{ kJ/mol}$)2829

**Limitations to the Octet Rule:**

- **Electron-Deficient (Fewer than 8 outer electrons):** Central atoms of Group 2 and 13 elements (e.g., $\text{BeH}_2$ with 4 electrons, $\text{BF}_3$ and $\text{AlCl}_3$ with 6 electrons)30more_horiz.
- **Expanded Octet (More than 8 outer electrons):** Central atoms from Period 3 onwards using vacant d-subshell orbitals (e.g., $\text{PF}_5$ with 10 electrons, $\text{SF}_6$ with 12 electrons)3033.

**4. Coordinate (Dative) Covalent Bonding**

- **Definition:** A covalent bond where **both electrons in the shared pair originate from the same atom** (the electron donor)3435.
- **Dative Bond Strength:** Once formed, a dative covalent bond is **identical in strength and length** to a normal covalent bond36.
- **Representation:** Indicated by an arrow ($\rightarrow$) pointing from the donor (electron-rich atom) to the acceptor (electron-deficient atom)3537.
- **Required Criteria:**
    1. **Donor:** Must have a **lone pair** of valence electrons in its outer shell (e.g., $\text{N}$ in $\text{NH}_3$)37.
    2. **Acceptor:** Must have a **vacant, empty orbital** in its valence shell to receive the pair (e.g., $\text{H}^+$ ion or $\text{B}$ in $\text{BF}_3$)3738.

**Core Examples to Draw and Memorize:**

- **Ammonium Ion (**$\text{NH}_4^+$**):** Nitrogen in ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) donates its lone pair to an electron-deficient hydrogen ion ($\text{H}^+$)2037: $$\text{H}_3\text{N:} + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \left[ \text{H}_3\text{N} \rightarrow \text{H} \right]^+$$
- **Ammonia-Boron Trifluoride (**$\text{H}_3\text{N}\rightarrow\text{BF}_3$**):** Nitrogen donates its lone pair to fill the vacant orbital of the electron-deficient Boron atom38: $$\text{H}_3\text{N:} + \text{BF}_3 \rightarrow \text{H}_3\text{N} \rightarrow \text{BF}_3$$
- **Carbon Monoxide (**$\text{C}\equiv\text{O}$ **/** $\text{C} \leftleftarrows \text{O}$**):** Oxygen forms two normal covalent bonds with Carbon. To allow Carbon to achieve an octet, Oxygen donates one of its lone pairs to form a third bond (the coordinate bond)3537.

**5. VSEPR Theory & Molecular Shapes**

- **Principle:** The shape of a molecule is determined by the total number of electron domains (bonding pairs and lone pairs) around the central atom3639. These domains **repel each other and position themselves as far apart as possible** to minimize repulsion36.
- **Repulsion Order:** $$\text{lone pair - lone pair} > \text{lone pair - bond pair} > \text{bond pair - bond pair}$$
    - _Effect:_ Lone pairs are held closer to the central atom's nucleus and exert greater repulsive force, **squeezing the bond pairs closer together** and decreasing bond angles by approximately $2^\circ \text{ to } 2.5^\circ$ per lone pair4041.

**6. Electronegativity, Bond Polarity & Molecular Polarity**

- **Electronegativity:** The relative ability of an atom to attract shared bonding electrons in a covalent bond4445.
- **Bond Polarity:**
    - **Non-polar Bond:** Equal sharing of electrons between atoms of identical electronegativity (e.g., $\text{Cl–Cl}$)46.
    - **Polar Bond:** Unequal sharing of electrons between atoms of different electronegativities4546. The more electronegative atom pulls the electron cloud closer, acquiring a **partial negative charge (**$\delta^-$**)**, while the less electronegative atom becomes **partial positive (**$\delta^+$**)**45. This separation of charge creates a **bond dipole**47.

**Deducing Molecular Polarity (The Cancellation Rule):**

A molecule containing polar bonds is only polar if its overall dipole moments do not cancel out4748. Use this step-by-step diagnostic pathway:

1. **Diatomic Molecules (2 Atoms):** Polar if the atoms are different (e.g., $\text{H–Cl}$ is polar); non-polar if the atoms are identical (e.g., $\text{Cl–Cl}$ is non-polar)47.
2. **Polyatomic Molecules (3+ Atoms):**
    - If the molecule is highly **symmetrical** (e.g., Linear $\text{CO}_2$, Trigonal Planar $\text{BF}_3$, Tetrahedral $\text{CCl}_4$) **and** all terminal atoms bonded to the central atom are identical, the individual bond dipoles cancel out symmetrically, making the overall molecule **non-polar**4748.
    - If the terminal atoms are different (e.g., $\text{CHCl}_3$ or $\text{BHCl}$), the dipoles do not cancel, making the molecule **polar**48.
    - If the shape is **asymmetrical** (e.g., V-shaped $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ or Trigonal Pyramidal $\text{NH}_3$), the bond dipoles **never cancel out**, making the molecule **polar** regardless of terminal atoms4849.

**7. Intermolecular Forces (IMFs)**

Intermolecular forces exist _between_ simple covalent molecules24. Their relative strengths for molecules of comparable size are: $$\text{London Dispersion Forces} < \text{Dipole-Dipole Forces} < \text{Hydrogen Bonds}$$2350

**A. London Dispersion (Induced Dipole-Induced Dipole) Forces**

- **Mechanism:** Constant motion of electrons in any molecule creates a temporary, instantaneous uneven distribution of charge, resulting in an **instantaneous dipole**5152. This dipole induces an opposing dipole in an adjacent molecule, creating a weak attractive force51.
- **Occurrence:** Present between **all** molecules (polar and non-polar)52. It is the **only** IMF holding non-polar molecules together52.
- **Key Relationship (Size & Electrons):** $$\text{More Electrons} \rightarrow \text{Larger Electron Cloud} \rightarrow \text{Cloud More Easily Distorted} \rightarrow \text{Stronger London Forces} \rightarrow \text{Higher B.P.}$$53
    - _Example:_ Boiling points of noble gases increase down the group ($\text{He} < \text{Ne} < \text{Ar} < \text{Kr}$) due to increasing electron counts52.

**B. Dipole-Dipole Forces**

- **Mechanism:** Electrostatic attraction between the permanent positive end ($\delta^+$) of one polar molecule and the permanent negative end ($\delta^-$) of an adjacent polar molecule53.
- **Occurrence:** Only between **polar molecules**53. Stronger than London forces for molecules of similar molar mass ($M_r$)2354.

**C. Hydrogen Bonding (The Strongest IMF)**

- **Criteria for Formation:**
    1. A hydrogen atom directly bonded to an extremely electronegative atom ($\text{N}$**,** $\text{O}$**, or** $\text{F}$), making the hydrogen highly electron-deficient54.
    2. An adjacent molecule with a highly electronegative atom ($\text{N}$**,** $\text{O}$**, or** $\text{F}$) containing at least one **lone pair** of electrons54.
- _Why N, O, F only?_ Their high electronegativity pulls electron density away from H, and their small atomic radius concentrates this charge, allowing the bare proton of Hydrogen to interact strongly with neighbouring lone pairs54.
- _Key Examples:_ Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$), Ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$), and Hydrogen Fluoride ($\text{HF}$)50.

**8. Giant Molecular (Covalent) Structures**

Unlike simple molecular structures, these macromolecules contain millions of atoms held together in a continuous, three-dimensional network of strong covalent bonds2055. No weak intermolecular forces are present56.

**A. Diamond (Carbon Allotrope)**

- **Structure:** Each Carbon atom is covalently bonded to **four other Carbon atoms** in a highly rigid, repeating **tetrahedral** arrangement57.
- **Properties:**
    - **Extremely High Melting Point:** Breaking millions of strong carbon-carbon covalent bonds requires vast amounts of thermal energy5658.
    - **Extremely Hard:** Rigid, tightly-bonded tetrahedral lattice resists deformation5859.
    - **Electrical Non-conductor:** All four valence electrons of each Carbon are localized in covalent bonds; there are **no delocalised electrons** to carry charge5859.
    - **Insoluble:** Insoluble in all solvents because carbon-carbon covalent bonds are too strong to be broken by solvent interactions58.

**B. Graphite (Carbon Allotrope)**

- **Structure:** Giant molecular structure arranged in flat, two-dimensional **hexagonal layers**60. Within the layers, each Carbon is bonded to **three other Carbon atoms**60. The individual layers are held together by **weak intermolecular forces**60.
- **Properties:**
    - **High Melting Point:** Strong covalent bonds within the hexagonal sheets require immense energy to break61.
    - **Soft and Slippery:** The layers can easily slide over one another because they are held together only by weak intermolecular forces60more_horiz. Used as a lubricant64.
    - **Good Electrical Conductor:** Each Carbon only uses 3 of its 4 valence electrons for bonding5961. The 4th valence electron is **delocalised** and free to move along the layers, acting as a charge carrier59more_horiz.

**C. Silicon Dioxide / Silica (**$\text{SiO}_2$**)**

- **Structure:** Giant molecular structure where each Silicon atom is covalently bonded to **four Oxygen atoms**, and each Oxygen atom is covalently bonded to **two Silicon atoms** in a giant tetrahedral network62.
- **Properties:** High melting point, non-conductor, hard, and insoluble (similar properties to Diamond)56.
