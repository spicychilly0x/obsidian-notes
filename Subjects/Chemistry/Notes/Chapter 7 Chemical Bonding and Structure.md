**1. Metallic Bonding & Giant Metallic Structure**

- **Definition:** The electrostatic force of attraction between a regular lattice of positive metal ions (cations) and a surrounding "sea" of mobile, delocalised valence electrons.
- **Structure:** Giant metallic lattice.
- **Factors Strengthening Metallic Bonds:**
    1. **Cationic Charge:** Cations with higher charges (e.g., $\text{Al}^{3+} > \text{Mg}^{2+} > \text{Na}^+$) contribute more delocalised electrons to the sea, strengthening the electrostatic attraction.
    2. **Cationic Radius:** Smaller cations allow the delocalised electrons to get closer to the positive nuclei, increasing bond strength.

**Physical Properties Explained:**

- **Electrical Conductivity:** Excellent conductors in **both solid and liquid/molten states** because the delocalised valence electrons are highly mobile and act as charge carriers to conduct electricity.
- **Thermal Conductivity:** Excellent conductors. Heated mobile electrons gain kinetic energy, move faster, and collide with neighbouring particles to transfer heat rapidly through the lattice.
- **Malleability & Ductility:** Metals can be hammered into sheets (malleable) or drawn into wires (ductile). When a force is applied, layers of orderly packed cations slide over one another without breaking the bond because the mobile "sea" of delocalised electrons moves with the sliding layers and maintains the attraction.
- **Melting & Boiling Points:** High to very high. Large amounts of thermal energy are required to overcome the strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the metal cations and the delocalised sea of electrons.

**Alloys (Mixtures of Metals):**

- **Definition:** A physical mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (e.g., Brass, Bronze, Stainless Steel, Duralumin).
- **Why Alloys are Harder than Pure Metals:**
    - In a pure metal, the atoms are of identical size and arranged in orderly rows, allowing them to slide past one another easily.
    - Alloying atoms of a **different size** disrupt the regular, orderly arrangement of rows of metal cations.
    - This **disruption prevents the layers from sliding easily** over one another, making the alloy much harder and stronger.
- **Melting Behavior:** Alloys do not have a sharp melting point but melt over a **range of temperatures**, which is typically **lower** than that of their constituent pure metals.

**2. Ionic Bonding & Giant Ionic Lattice**

- **Definition:** The electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions (cations and anions) formed by the complete transfer of valence electrons from metal to non-metal atoms.
- **Structure:** Giant ionic lattice containing a regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions.

**Physical Properties Explained:**

- **Melting & Boiling Points:** Very high. A massive amount of thermal energy is required to overcome the strong, extensive electrostatic forces of attraction holding the oppositely charged ions together in the lattice.
- **Electrical Conductivity:**
    - **Solid State:** Non-conductors because the ions are locked in fixed positions in the lattice and are **immobile**.
    - **Molten (l) or Aqueous (aq) States:** Excellent conductors because the rigid lattice breaks down, freeing the **ions to become mobile** and migrate towards electrodes to carry electric current.
- **Brittleness:** Highly brittle. When an external force is applied, layers of ions slide slightly. This brings **ions of like charges adjacent to one another** (positive next to positive, negative next to negative), resulting in strong electrostatic repulsion that shatters the lattice.
- **Solubility:** Generally soluble in polar solvents (like water) because the polar water molecules attract the ions on the lattice surface, releasing enough energy to disrupt the ionic bonds. Generally insoluble in non-polar organic solvents.

**3. Covalent Bonding & Simple Molecular Substances**

- **Definition:** The electrostatic force of attraction between shared pairs of bonding electrons and the positively charged nuclei of the non-metal atoms.
- **Structure:** Simple molecular structure containing discrete molecules.
- **The Crucial Exam Distinction:**
    - **Intramolecular Forces:** Extremely **strong covalent bonds** _within_ the molecules. These are **Simultaneously never** broken during phase changes (melting/boiling).
    - **Intermolecular Forces:** Very **weak forces of attraction** _between_ the molecules. Only these weak forces are overcome during melting and boiling.
- **Bond Energy:** The energy required to break one mole of a specific covalent bond in the gaseous state.
    - _Key Relationship:_ Triple bonds share more electrons, creating a greater nuclear pull. This results in a shorter bond length and a **higher bond energy** than double or single bonds:
        - Single bond: $\text{F-F}$ ($154\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$)
        - Double bond: $\text{O=O}$ ($495\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$)
        - Triple bond: $\text{N}\equiv\text{N}$ ($941\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$)
- **Octet Rule Exceptions:**
    - **Electron-Deficient (Fewer than 8 valence electrons):** Occurs in central atoms of Group 2 and 13 elements (e.g., $\text{BeH}_2$ with 4 outer electrons; $\text{BF}_3$ and $\text{AlCl}_3$ with 6 outer electrons).
    - **Expanded Octet (More than 8 valence electrons):** Central atoms from Period 3 onwards can expand their octet due to the availability of **vacant d-subshells** (e.g., $\text{PF}_5$ with 10 outer electrons; $\text{SF}_6$ with 12 outer electrons).

**4. Coordinate (Dative) Covalent Bonding**

- **Definition:** A covalent bond in which **both shared electrons in the bonding pair originate from the same atom** (the donor).
- **Properties:** Once formed, a dative covalent bond has the exact same strength and length as a normal covalent bond.
- **Requirements for Formation:**
    1. **Donor Atom:** Must possess at least one **lone pair** of electrons in its valence shell.
    2. **Acceptor Atom/Ion:** Must have an **empty (vacant) orbital** in its valence shell to accommodate the incoming pair of electrons.
- **Common Examples to Memorize:**
    - **Ammonium Ion (**$\text{NH}_4^+$**):** The Nitrogen atom in an ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) molecule donates its lone pair to a vacant orbital in an electron-deficient hydrogen ion ($\text{H}^+$).
    - **Ammonia-Boron Trifluoride (**$\text{H}_3\text{N}\rightarrow\text{BF}_3$**):** Nitrogen in $\text{NH}_3$ donates its lone pair to the vacant orbital of the electron-deficient Boron atom in $\text{BF}_3$.
    - **Carbon Monoxide (**$\text{C}\equiv\text{O}$ **or** $\text{C}\leftleftarrows\text{O}$**):** Oxygen forms two normal covalent bonds with carbon, and then donates one of its lone pairs to carbon to form a coordinate bond, allowing carbon to complete its stable octet.

**5. VSEPR Theory & Molecular Shapes**

- **Core Principle:** The shape of a molecule is determined by the total number of electron domains (bonding pairs and lone pairs) surrounding the central atom. Because electron domains are negatively charged, they **repel each other and position themselves as far apart as possible** to minimize repulsion.
- **Repulsion Strengths:** $$\text{Lone Pair - Lone Pair} > \text{Lone Pair - Bond Pair} > \text{Bond Pair - Bond Pair}$$
    - _Effect:_ Because lone pairs are held closer to the central atom's nucleus, they exert a greater repulsive force. This forces the adjacent bonding pairs closer together, **reducing the bond angle by approximately** $2^\circ$ **to** $2.5^\circ$ **per lone pair**.
- _Note:_ For VSEPR shape determination, multiple covalent bonds (double or triple) are treated as **one single electron domain**.

**6. Electronegativity, Bond Polarity & Molecular Polarity**

- **Electronegativity:** The relative measure of an atom's ability to attract the shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
- **Bond Polarity:**
    - **Non-polar Bond:** Shared electrons are distributed equally between atoms of identical electronegativity (e.g., $\text{Cl-Cl}$).
    - **Polar Bond:** Unequal sharing of bonding electrons due to different electronegativities. The more electronegative atom pulls the shared pair closer, gaining a partial negative charge ($\delta^-$), while the less electronegative atom gains a partial positive charge ($\delta^+$). This creates a **dipole**.
- **Determining Molecular Polarity (The Symmetry Rule):**
    - **Symmetrical Molecules:** If a molecule is highly symmetrical (e.g., Linear $\text{CO}_2$, Trigonal Planar $\text{BF}_3$, Tetrahedral $\text{CCl}_4$) **and** all terminal atoms are identical, the individual polar bond dipoles cancel each other out, making the overall molecule **non-polar**.
    - **Asymmetrical Molecules:** If the terminal atoms are different (e.g., $\text{CHCl}_3$) or the molecule is asymmetrical (e.g., V-shaped $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, Trigonal Pyramidal $\text{NH}_3$), the dipoles do not cancel out, resulting in a **polar** molecule.

**7. Intermolecular Forces (IMFs) in Simple Molecules**

Intermolecular forces exist only _between_ simple molecular substances. Their relative strengths (for molecules of similar mass) are: $$\text{London Dispersion Forces (LDF)} < \text{Dipole-Dipole Forces} < \text{Hydrogen Bonds}$$

**A. London Dispersion Forces (LDF):**

- **Mechanism:** Constant movement of electrons creates a temporary, instantaneous uneven distribution of charge in an atom or molecule, producing an **instantaneous dipole**. This dipole induces an opposing dipole in an adjacent molecule, creating a weak electrostatic attraction.
- **Occurrence:** Present in **all** molecules (polar and non-polar). It is the **only** force holding non-polar molecules together.
- **Key Relationship (Safely Formatted Block):**

```
More Electrons -> Larger Electron Cloud -> More Polarisable Cloud -> Stronger LDF -> Higher Boiling Point
```

- _Example:_ Boiling points of Group 17 elements increase down the group ($\text{F}_2 < \text{Cl}_2 < \text{Br}_2 < \text{I}_2$) as electron count increases.

**B. Dipole-Dipole Forces:**

- **Mechanism:** Electrostatic attraction between permanent dipoles of adjacent **polar molecules** (the $\delta^+$ end of one molecule attracts the $\delta^-$ end of another).

**C. Hydrogen Bonding (Strongest IMF):**

- **Criteria for Formation:**
    1. A hydrogen atom must be directly bonded to an extremely electronegative atom ($\text{N}$, $\text{O}$, or $\text{F}$), rendering the hydrogen highly electron-deficient.
    2. An adjacent molecule must have an $\text{N}$, $\text{O}$, or $\text{F}$ atom with a **lone pair** of electrons to attract the electron-deficient hydrogen.
    3. _Examples:_ $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{NH}_3$, and $\text{HF}$.

**8. Giant Molecular (Covalent) Structures**

These substances do not consist of discrete molecules. Instead, they exist as a massive, continuous three-dimensional network of atoms held together entirely by strong covalent bonds.

**A. Diamond (Carbon Allotrope):**

- **Structure:** Each Carbon atom is covalently bonded to **four other Carbon atoms** in a rigid, three-dimensional **tetrahedral** arrangement.
- **Properties:** Extremely high melting point (requires vast energy to break millions of strong covalent bonds), extremely hard, insoluble in all solvents, and a **non-conductor** because all valence electrons are locked in localized covalent bonds (no mobile charge carriers).

**B. Graphite (Carbon Allotrope):**

- **Structure:** Arranged in flat, parallel two-dimensional layers of **hexagonal rings**. Within each layer, each Carbon is bonded to **three other Carbon atoms**. The layers are held together by **weak intermolecular forces**.
- **Properties:** High melting point (due to strong covalent bonds within layers), soft and slippery (layers can slide over each other easily because the weak intermolecular forces between layers are easily overcome; used as a lubricant), and an **excellent electrical conductor** because each carbon uses only 3 of its 4 valence electrons for bonding, leaving one **delocalised electron** per carbon atom free to move along the layers to carry current.

**C. Silicon Dioxide / Silica (**$\text{SiO}_2$**):**

- **Structure:** A giant covalent tetrahedral structure where each Silicon atom is covalently bonded to **four Oxygen atoms**, and each Oxygen atom is bonded to **two Silicon atoms**.
- **Properties:** Extremely high melting point, hard, insoluble, and a non-conductor of electricity (similar physical properties to Diamond).

**⚠️ Critical Exam Pitfalls & Grading Standards (Must Memorize):**

- **The "Conductivity" Explanation Trap:** Never write "conducts electricity because of free-moving electrons/ions" without specifying state.
    - For **ionic compounds**, explicitly state that they only conduct in **molten or aqueous states because the giant lattice breaks down, freeing the ions to become mobile**.
    - For **metals/graphite**, explicitly state they conduct in **both solid and liquid states due to the presence of mobile, delocalised valence electrons**.
- **The "Melting Point" Explanation Trap:** When explaining why simple covalent molecular compounds have low melting points, never say "covalent bonds are weak." Always specify that **very little energy is required to overcome the weak intermolecular forces of attraction** between the molecules, and that the **strong covalent bonds within the molecules are not broken**.
- **Silicon Dioxide formula explanation:** If asked why silicon dioxide is a solid and carbon dioxide is a gas at room temperature, explain that **silicon dioxide has a giant covalent structure** requiring massive energy to break strong covalent bonds, whereas **carbon dioxide has a simple molecular structure** where only weak intermolecular forces need to be overcome.
