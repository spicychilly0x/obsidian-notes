# 1. THE SPEAKER

### Point 1 — She is a bearer of inherited trauma, carrying her grandfather's prison-camp suffering as her own living history.

> _"Grandfather spent the War / in a prison camp"_ _"brothers, fathers, uncles / disappeared"_

The speaker does not describe her grandfather's experience as distant family lore; she absorbs it into her own identity. The **enumeration** of male relatives (**"brothers, fathers, uncles"**) creates a generational chain of loss that binds her to the past. The **diction** of **"disappeared"** carries the specific horror of wartime erasure. The impression is of a woman whose present is haunted by the ghosts of her family's unacknowledged suffering.

### Point 2 — She is performatively polite yet inwardly furious, forcing a smile that conceals historical grievance.

> _"you stare / smile vacantly empty, not genuine"_ _"I return your smile"_

The **juxtaposition** between the tourist's **"vacantly empty"** smile and the speaker's deliberate **"return"** reveals a social ritual of mutual pretense. The **diction** of **"vacantly empty"** suggests the tourist's ignorance, while the speaker's smile is an act of obligation rather than warmth. The impression is of a woman trapped in a performance of hospitality while internally confronting an enemy of historical amnesia.

### Point 3 — She is an archivist of erased atrocities, remembering what the tourist's textbooks have deliberately omitted.

> _"but Changi, Nanking, / and death railways / comfort girls, / are not recounted / in your texts"_ _"at SGH / a simple cross / speaks of the slaughter / of civilians"_

The **enumeration** of **"Changi, Nanking, death railways, comfort girls"** functions as an alternative curriculum, a counter-history to the tourist's education. The **diction** of **"not recounted"** accuses not just the tourist but an entire pedagogical system of deliberate erasure. The **symbolism** of the **"simple cross"** at SGH transforms a medical marker into a war memorial. The impression is of a woman who acts as a human monument, preserving memory that official histories have buried.

### Point 4 — She is a universalist who collapses the distance between victim and perpetrator, recognizing shared human complicity.

> _"and it is you / and it is i!"_ _"but that was neither you nor i"_

The **anaphora** of **"and it is"** (and its earlier negation **"neither you nor i"**) creates a rhythmic oscillation between separation and unity. The **exclamation mark** after **"i!"** transforms the line into a cry of recognition — both are implicated in the cycle. The impression is of a speaker who refuses simple blame, instead locating both herself and the tourist inside a shared, broken humanity.

# 2. THE JAPANESE TOURIST

### Point 1 — They are a superficial sightseer, reducing complex history to a "vacation smile" snapped from a tour bus.

> _"you stare / smile vacantly empty, not genuine / from your bus / - a vacation smile"_ _"at fifty now, / what would you have known?"_

The **diction** of **"vacantly empty"** is a double negation that hollows out the tourist's expression — there is nothing behind the teeth. The **prepositional phrase** **"from your bus"** emphasizes physical and emotional distance; they observe without entering. The **rhetorical question** **"what would you have known?"** is not a genuine inquiry but a dismissal — at fifty, they should know, yet they don't. The impression is of a comfortable, middle-aged consumer of history who treats trauma as scenery.

### Point 2 — They are complicit in generational amnesia, raising children on a diet of selective victimhood.

> _"about your children / only being shown / the work / of the mushroom cloud"_ _"and your children / need only / remember Hiroshima"_

The **diction** of **"only"** (used twice) exposes the brutal narrowness of their education — the atomic bomb and nothing else. The **metonymy** of the **"mushroom cloud"** reduces the entire Pacific War to a single image of Japanese victimhood. The **monostitch** ending **"remember Hiroshima"** functions as a chilling pedagogical command. The impression is of a parent who passes down blindness, ensuring the next generation remains as ignorant as the current one.

### Point 3 — They are shielded by national myth, aesthetically insulated from their own country's brutality by romantic imagery.

> _"sonorous names / invoking lovely images / - paper cranes proclaiming peace / - Kyoto overlaid with snow / - cherry blossoms, green tea and bento"_ _"but that was neither you nor i"_

The **enumeration** of lovely images creates a seductive catalogue of cultural soft power — Japan as a brand of beauty and peace. The **auditory imagery** of **"sonorous names"** suggests these words are chanted like a mantra. The **juxtaposition** with **"but that was neither you nor i"** shatters the myth: the tourist enjoys the lovely packaging while denying the violence that funded it. The impression is of a person wrapped in a national identity so beautiful it functions as armor against truth.

# 3. THE GRANDFATHER

### Point 1 — He is dehumanized by systematic deprivation, his survival reduced to a cruel mathematics of rations.

> _"survived / on green bean rations / exchanged at 2:1 for his / suffering rice"_ _"in a prison camp"_

The **diction** of **"exchanged at 2:1"** transforms human survival into a transaction, a ratio that strips the grandfather of dignity and reduces him to a unit of accounting. The **transferred epithet** of **"suffering rice"** suggests that even his food was in pain — or that his suffering was so total it infected everything he touched. The impression is of a man who was not merely imprisoned but algorithmically starved.

### Point 2 — He is a victim of total domestic erasure, returning from the camp to find his home destroyed.

> _"returning to a looted home"_ _"brothers, fathers, uncles / disappeared"_

The **participle** **"returning"** suggests an ongoing, never-completed journey — he can never truly arrive home because home no longer exists. The **enumeration** of lost male relatives creates a portrait of a family tree that has been chainsawed. The **diction** of **"looted"** implies not just theft but violation; the domestic sanctuary has been ransacked. The impression is of a man who survived the camp only to discover that the war had already taken everything he was fighting to return to.

### Point 3 — He is a ghost in the national narrative, present in family memory but erased from the tourist's textbooks.

> _"Grandfather spent the War / in a prison camp"_ _"are not recounted / in your texts"_

The **juxtaposition** between the speaker's intimate knowledge of her grandfather and the tourist's **"texts"** that exclude him creates a hierarchy of memory — some stories are told at dinner tables, others are deleted from history books. The impression is of a man who exists in family lore but has been made invisible by official narratives.

# 4. THE GRANDMOTHER

### Point 1 — She is traumatized into instantaneous aging, her body recording grief that words cannot express.

> _"Grandmother's hair / turned white"_ _"hyperbole"_ (as the poem self-annotates)

The **hyperbole** of hair turning white from shock is a classical trope for extreme trauma, but the poem's self-aware notation suggests even this may be understatement rather than exaggeration. The **visual imagery** of white hair against dark grief creates a stark, photographic contrast. The impression is of a woman whose body has been rewritten by war, her biology hijacked by sorrow.

### Point 2 — She is a silent, stationary witness to male suffering, her voice absent while her appearance speaks volumes.

> _"Grandmother's hair / turned white"_ _"brothers, fathers, uncles / disappeared"_

While the men are **enumerated** and named by relationship, the grandmother is identified only by her altered appearance. The **juxtaposition** between the list of vanished men and her single, physical transformation suggests that women's suffering in war is visible but unvoiced. The impression is of a woman who bears the domestic cost of war without being granted a narrative of her own.

### Point 3 — She is the keeper of a ruined home, her body becoming the last intact monument after the house is looted.

> _"returning to a looted home"_ _"Grandmother's hair / turned white"_

The **symbolism** of the **"looted home"** and the grandmother's white hair creates a parallel: the house has been emptied of its contents, and her body has been emptied of its color. Both are sites of invasion and loss. The impression is of a woman who has become a walking memorial, her very presence a reminder of what was stolen.

# 5. HIROSHIMA / NAGASAKI (as place and symbol)

### Point 1 — They are aesthetically rebranded, their names now invoking peace and beauty rather than war responsibility.

> _"sonorous names / invoking lovely images / - paper cranes proclaiming peace / - Kyoto overlaid with snow / - cherry blossoms, green tea and bento"_ _"Nagasaki" / "Hiroshima"_

The **auditory imagery** of **"sonorous names"** treats these cities as musical notes rather than graveyards. The **enumeration** of lovely images creates a seductive **juxtaposition** — the names of bombed cities are now attached to tourist attractions. The impression is of places that have been cosmetically transformed from symbols of war into brands of cultural charm.

### Point 2 — They are presented as the exclusive focus of Japanese war memory, erasing all other Pacific suffering.

> _"Only Hiroshima"_ (title) _"about your children / only being shown / the work / of the mushroom cloud"_

The title **"Only Hiroshima"** functions as a **monostitch** and a thesis statement — the word **"Only"** reduces an entire nation's war experience to a single act of victimhood. The **diction** of **"only being shown"** reveals a pedagogical strategy of deliberate narrowing. The impression is of cities that have become black holes of memory, sucking in all attention while surrounding atrocities vanish.

### Point 3 — They are trapped in victimhood, unable to escape a narrative that prevents acknowledgment of their own nation's crimes.

> _"remember Hiroshima"_ _"if you will only tell / of Nagasaki"_

The **imperative** **"remember"** functions as a command to the tourist's children — a liturgical instruction in national martyrdom. The **conditional** **"if you will only tell"** suggests that the speaker sees this selective telling as a choice, not an accident. The impression is of cities that are not just places but ideological prisons, locking their inhabitants inside a single story of suffering.

# 6. THE MUSHROOM CLOUD

### Point 1 — It is a metonymic monster, standing in for the entire Pacific War while erasing every other horror.

> _"only being shown / the work / of the mushroom cloud"_ _"the work / of the mushroom cloud"_

The **metonymy** of the **"mushroom cloud"** reduces the atomic bombings to a single, spectacular image — iconic, terrifying, and utterly detached from context. The **diction** of **"work"** is chillingly clinical, as if the cloud were a laborer or an artist rather than an instrument of mass death. The impression is of a symbol so powerful it functions as a screen, blocking out all other war memories.

### Point 2 — It is the sole visual text of the tourist's education, a photographic icon that replaces historical complexity.

> _"only being shown / the work / of the mushroom cloud"_ _"but Changi, Nanking, / and death railways / comfort girls, / are not recounted"_

The **juxtaposition** between the visible **"mushroom cloud"** and the unrecounted Asian atrocities creates a hierarchy of memory — some deaths are photographed, others are deleted. The **diction** of **"shown"** versus **"recounted"** reveals the power of the image over the word; the cloud is seen, while other suffering is silenced. The impression is of a symbol that has become a weapon of historical amnesia.

### Point 3 — It is a false endpoint, presented as the war's climax while the speaker insists the violence continued elsewhere.

> _"the work / of the mushroom cloud"_ _"the thrusts - / of brutes / and bayonets goes on"_

The **metonymy** of the cloud suggests a definitive, singular event — the war ended with the bomb. But the speaker counters this with the **kinaesthetic imagery** of **"thrusts"** and **"bayonets,"** which are ongoing, bodily, and pre-modern. The **diction** of **"goes on"** explicitly rejects the cloud as an ending. The impression is of a symbol that falsely promises closure while the real violence — the bayonets, the camps, the rapes — continues to be ignored.

# 7. THE LITANY OF ATROCITIES (Somalia, Romania, Rwanda, Serbia, Pol Pot, Amin, Apartheid)

### Point 1 — They are an endless, repeating cycle of human cruelty that mocks the promise of "Never Again."

> _"Somalia / Romania / Rwanda / Serbia / Pol Pot / Amin / Apartheid / the litany goes on"_ _"Never Again!" / is Auschwitz's strident cry / but the thrusts - / of brutes / and bayonets goes on"_

The **enumeration** creates a drumbeat of horror — each name is a separate wound, yet together they form a pattern. The **allusion** to **"Never Again"** and **"Auschwitz"** frames the list within the context of failed promises. The **diction** of **"litany"** suggests a religious recitation, as if these atrocities are now part of a catechism of despair. The impression is of history not as progress but as a stuck record of violence.

### Point 2 — They collapse the moral distance between "you" and "i," making both tourist and speaker inhabitants of a violent world.

> _"and it is you / and it is i!"_ _"the litany goes on"_

The **anaphora** of **"and it is"** (repeated twice) creates a rhythmic, inescapable equation — victim and witness, perpetrator and survivor, are all trapped in the same list. The **exclamation mark** transforms the line into a cry of recognition or accusation. The impression is of a universal human condition in which no one is innocent and everyone is implicated.

### Point 3 — They are presented as ongoing and bodily, rejecting the idea that modernity has outgrown barbarism.

> _"but the thrusts - / of brutes / and bayonets goes on"_ _"the litany goes on"_

The **kinaesthetic imagery** of **"thrusts"** makes violence physical and intimate — not distant or technological like the mushroom cloud, but hand-to-hand and savage. The **diction** of **"brutes"** is archaic, suggesting that civilization has not evolved beyond primal cruelty. The repetition of **"goes on"** creates a **refrain** of hopelessness. The impression is of a world where the bayonet, not the bomb, is the truest symbol of humanity's endless capacity for harm.
