**12 Literary Devices (2 Quotes Each)**

**1. Juxtaposition / Direct Contrast**

- **Quote 1:** `"You would rather run than walk, rather climb than run"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 1, describing the 9-year-old's restless physical energy and desire for movement.
    - **Theme Link:** Juxtaposes the child's boundless physical vitality against the adult's cautious restraint, showing how growing up destroys spontaneous freedom.
- **Quote 2:** `"careful of a bad back or a bruised foot."`
    - **Context:** Stanza 2, where the adult speaker details her present physical injuries and physical decline.
    - **Theme Link:** Contrasts her current broken body against the child's fearless agility, highlighting the painful physical toll of ageing.

---

**2. Irony (Situational & Character Irony)**

- **Quote 1:** `"I'd like to say that we could be friends"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 4, where the adult speaker addresses her younger self directly.
    - **Theme Link (Situational Irony):** It is deeply ironic that the adult longs to reconnect with her past self, yet realizes they are so distant they cannot even be friends.
- **Quote 2:** `"slowly peeling a ripe scab from your knee"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 6, as the speaker observes the child picking at her injury in deep focus.
    - **Theme Link (Situational Irony):** It is ironic that picking a wound (`"ripe scab"`) brings satisfaction, showing that experiencing physical pain is a natural part of youth's fearless vitality.

---

**3. Metaphor**

- **Quote 1:** `"I have spoiled this body we once shared."`
    - **Context:** Stanza 2, opening line where the adult speaker assesses her physical state.
    - **Theme Link:** Metaphorically frames ageing as "spoiling" a shared vessel, conveying adult guilt over the loss of youthful health and perfection.
- **Quote 2:** `"slowly peeling a ripe scab from your knee"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 6, describing the child absorbed in picking her scab.
    - **Theme Link:** The `"ripe scab"` acts as a metaphor for maturing and life's wounds—showing that growing up requires tasting life's bittersweet experiences.

---

**4. Apostrophe (Direct Address to Past Self)**

- **Quote 1:** `"You must forgive me. Don't look so surprised,"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 1, line 1, where the adult speaker begins her one-sided dialogue with her 9-year-old self.
    - **Theme Link:** Using apostrophe directly confronts the past self, establishing her deep regret and desire for forgiveness regarding her current aged body.
- **Quote 2:** `"I have spoiled this body we once shared."`
    - **Context:** Stanza 2, directly addressing the child about their shared physical form.
    - **Theme Link:** Direct address highlights the internal self-division between who she was as a child and who she has become as an adult.

---

**5. Symbolism & Central Motif**

- **Quote 1:** `"as the white paper to write it on."`
    - **Context:** Stanza 3, describing the childhood ambition and unwritten future.
    - **Theme Link:** `"White paper"` symbolises pristine, uncorrupted innocence and limitless potential that time eventually overwrites with adult realities.
- **Quote 2:** `"into the summer morning?"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 2, asking if the child remembers leaping out the window into the warm morning.
    - **Theme Link:** `"Summer morning"` acts as a central motif for the warmth, brightness, and boundless optimism of childhood.

---

**6. Kinesthetic & Tactile Imagery**

- **Quote 1:** `"rather leap from a height than anything."`
    - **Context:** Stanza 1, ending line of the opening description of the child.
    - **Theme Link:** Dynamic kinesthetic imagery (`"leap from a height"`) captures the child's fearless relationship with physical risk and gravity.
- **Quote 2:** `"careful of a bad back or a bruised foot."`
    - **Context:** Stanza 2, describing the adult's cautious movement.
    - **Theme Link:** Tactile imagery (`"bad back"`, `"bruised foot"`) gives visceral reality to adult degeneration and physical pain.

---

**7. Gustatory Imagery (Taste)**

- **Quote 1:** `"a baby vole, or a bag of sherbet lemons —"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 3, listing childhood distractions and treats.
    - **Theme Link:** Gustatory imagery (`"sherbet lemons"`) evokes the sweet, tangy innocence of childhood pleasure before adult anxieties took over.
- **Quote 2:** `"to taste it on your tongue."`
    - **Context:** Final line of the poem, describing the child tasting the scab.
    - **Theme Link:** Visceral gustatory imagery (`"taste it on your tongue"`) illustrates the child's uninhibited, sensory exploration of life and pain.

---

**8. Transferred Epithet (Hypallage)**

- **Quote 1:** `"time to hide down scared lanes"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 4, listing childhood memories of playing near dark roads.
    - **Theme Link:** Transfers the human emotion (`"scared"`) onto the `"lanes"`, conveying how sinister adult threats begin to invade and shadow childhood places.
- **Quote 2:** `"I leave you in an ecstasy of concentration"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 6, describing the child completely immersed in her own world.
    - **Theme Link:** Transfers the emotional state (`"ecstasy"`) to define her mental state of `"concentration"`, showing the pure, unbothered joy of childhood focus.

---

**9. Anaphora / Parallel Syntax**

- **Quote 1:** `"You would rather run than walk, rather climb than run"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 1, describing the child's escalation of movement.
    - **Theme Link:** Parallel structure repeating `"rather"` emphasizes the child's relentless desire to push physical limits and move faster.
- **Quote 2:** `"time to hide down scared lanes"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 4, repeating the word `"time"` across lines 25–26.
    - **Theme Link:** Anaphora of `"time"` stresses the relentless march of time that forces childhood adventure to yield to adult danger.

---

**10. Tone (Apologetic vs. Accepting)**

- **Quote 1:** `"You must forgive me. Don't look so surprised,"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 1, line 1, opening the poem.
    - **Theme Link (Apologetic Tone):** An apologetic, penitent tone establishes her remorse for failing to preserve the child's physical perfection.
- **Quote 2:** `"but the truth is we have nothing in common"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 4, accepting the reality of their separation.
    - **Theme Link (Accepting Tone):** A sober, realistic tone acknowledges that time has created an unbridgeable gulf between adult and child.

---

**11. Structural Volta / Turning Point**

- **Quote 1:** `"I'd like to say that we could be friends"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 4, line 22, where the speaker shifts from nostalgic longing to realistic assessment.
    - **Theme Link:** Serves as a major structural volta, turning away from futile nostalgia toward accepting that the adult and child belong to different worlds.
- **Quote 2:** `"but no, I shan't cloud your morning. God knows"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 5, line 32, where the speaker decides not to burden the child.
    - **Theme Link:** Structural turn where the adult actively stops intruding, choosing to preserve the child's joy rather than inflict adult fears.

---

**12. Alliteration & Sound Devices (Plosives vs. Soft Sibilance)**

- **Quote 1:** `"careful of a bad back or a bruised foot."`
    - **Context:** Stanza 2, describing physical ailments.
    - **Theme Link (Harsh Plosives):** Heavy plosive **'b'** sounds (`"bad back... bruised"`) create a thudding, painful rhythm that mimics bodily injury and weariness.
- **Quote 2:** `"slowly peeling a ripe scab from your knee"`
    - **Context:** Stanza 6, as the speaker watches the child.
    - **Theme Link (Sibilance & Soft Sounds):** Soft **'s'** and liquid **'l'** sounds (`"slowly... scab"`) create a hushed, gentle rhythm reflecting the quiet, undisturbed sanctuary of childhood play.
