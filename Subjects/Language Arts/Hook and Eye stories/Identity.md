# **Master Literary Device Arsenal for** **Identity**

---

### **1. Dialogue & Speech Patterns (Epizeuxis, Abrupt Interrogatives, Imperatives)**

- **Point 1 (Societal):** Curt, interrogative dialogue and epizeuxis (rapid repetition) from locals enforce her status as an unwanted alien[1][2].
    - **Quote 1:** `"YOU India?" ... "No... no..." "Sri Lanka?"`[3]
    - **Quote 2:** `"You say you're a Singaporean. But you don't know Malay?"`[3]
    - **Analysis:** The driver's abrupt, repetitive probing (`"YOU India?"`) acts as linguistic gatekeeping, reducing her to a racial category and mocking her national identity.
- **Point 2 (Domestic):** Imperative, commanding dialogue within the household strips her of maternal and personal dignity[2][4].
    - **Quote 1:** `"Hey, I don't want your thosai," her husband's voice commanded from the hall. "Make me some French toast."`[4][5]
    - **Quote 2:** `"Go on, Mum, you wouldn't know anything."`[6]
    - **Analysis:** Imperative verbs (`"Make me"`) and dismissive statements weaponise household speech to subordinate her as an unpaid servant.

---

### **2. Status-Laden Diction & Derogatory Epithets**

- **Point 1 (Societal):** Class-bound, racialised diction immediately categorises Indian women into foreign domestic worker roles[2][7].
    - **Quote 1:** `"You come from India? Come to do housework?"`[3]
    - **Quote 2:** `"From India means must be maid?"`[3]
    - **Analysis:** The noun `"maid"` functions as a status-laden label through which Singaporean society erases her postgraduate degree and citizenship[1][2].
- **Point 2 (Domestic):** Derogatory epithets used by her own family weaponise her foreign origins to undermine her authority[2][8].
    - **Quote 1:** `"If I were like you, people would also tease me as a country bumpkin from India."`[6][8]
    - **Quote 2:** `"I am sick of this daily routine of thosai, iddili and chutney. Like a beggar."`[5]
    - **Analysis:** Insulting epithets (`"country bumpkin"`, `"beggar"`) align her cultural identity with backwardness, devaluing her presence in her own home[4][8].

---

### **3. The Mirror & Reflection Motif**

- **Point 1 (Societal):** The bathroom mirror forces a painful confrontation with the low-status racial stereotype projected onto her by society[9][10].
    - **Quote 1:** `"A dark, skinny body. Short hair... Do I look like an Indian or Sri Lankan maid?"`[11]
    - **Quote 2:** `"With her dishevelled hair and face drenched in perspiration, she did look like a maid."`[11]
    - **Analysis:** Visual reflection in the mirror reveals how societal prejudice penetrates her psyche, forcing her to internalise the physical degradation caused by non-stop labor[9][10].
- **Point 2 (Domestic):** The dressing table mirror becomes a site of intergenerational conflict and cultural rejection[2][8].
    - **Quote 1:** `"Suba, standing in front of the mirror and painstakingly applying dark red lipstick."`[6]
    - **Quote 2:** `"Glaring at her mother in anger, Suba threw her comb down onto the dressing table."`[6]
    - **Analysis:** The mirror frames her daughter assimilating into Westernised beauty norms (`"dark red lipstick"`), reflecting the mother's inability to pass down traditional values[2][8].

---

### **4. Symbolism & Material Culture (NRIC, Food, Jewellery)**

- **Point 1 (Societal):** Official state symbols highlight the illusion of legal equality vs. lived discrimination[2][12].
    - **Quote 1:** `"Her distracted eyes fell on the pink Singapore identity card placed neatly in the purse pocket."`[13]
    - **Quote 2:** `"It seemed to smile at her smugly."`[13]
    - **Analysis:** The Pink NRIC symbolises official citizenship, yet its "smug smile" highlights that a government card cannot grant social acceptance[2][12].
- **Point 2 (Domestic):** Food motifs and traditional jewellery symbolise cultural erasure and marital exploitation[4][9].
    - **Quote 1:** `"Why is the thosai like this, dry and tough?... Make me some French toast."`[5]
    - **Quote 2:** `"Apart from the yellow thread that was her thaali, the marital necklace... her only other jewellery was a thin chain"`[11]
    - **Analysis:** Rejecting traditional _thosai_ for Western _French toast_ symbolises her family's cultural rejection, while her plain _thaali_ highlights how marriage brought servitude rather than security[4][9].

---

### **5. Irony (Situational, Character, & Structural)**

- **Point 1 (Societal):** Situational irony exposes how holding legal citizenship and advanced degrees fails to protect her from low-status stereotyping[1][14].
    - **Quote 1:** `"Your father has only A-Levels. I have passed MSc Maths."`[6]
    - **Quote 2:** `"You studied in India. You wouldn't understand a thing about Maths in Singapore."`[6]
    - **Analysis:** Deeply ironic that her postgraduate qualification is dismissed by her own son simply because it was awarded in India[14][15].
- **Point 2 (Domestic):** Character irony reveals how her husband selected her for traditional submissiveness, only to abuse her for her cultural background[15][16].
    - **Quote 1:** `"Don't forget that I married a girl from India because I like my wife to be conservative and feminine."`[17]
    - **Quote 2:** `"openly stating that her "India ways" were not suitable"`[4][15]
    - **Analysis:** Her husband specifically sought an Indian bride for traditional virtues, yet hypocritically berates her "India ways" to strip her authority[4][15].

---

### **6. Juxtaposition & Direct Contrast**

- **Point 1 (Societal):** Juxtaposes her internal self-identification as a citizen against society's refusal to integrate her[2][12].
    - **Quote 1:** `"I am a Singaporean."`[13]
    - **Quote 2:** `"She had no such intimate friends here. Anyway, weddings in Singapore were no fun."`[13]
    - **Analysis:** Contrasts her proud legal claim (`"I am a Singaporean"`) against her real-world social isolation in Singapore[2][13].
- **Point 2 (Domestic):** Juxtaposes her past warm community life in India against her present domestic servitude[4][10].
    - **Quote 1:** `"She thought about the weddings she had attended back home in India. What riotous fun!"`[13]
    - **Quote 2:** `"It never occurred to her mother-in-law to ever lift a finger and help her with the chores."`[5]
    - **Analysis:** Contrasts the joy of her past life in India with the exhausting, unappreciated housework demanded by her in-laws[4][10].

---

### **7. Characterisation (Protagonist vs. Antagonists)**

- **Point 1 (Protagonist):** Characterised as a long-suffering, self-sacrificing woman whose physical and mental health are eroded by unpaid labor[8][9].
    - **Quote 1:** `"She was exhausted and had gone to bed without completing her chores..."`[5]
    - **Quote 2:** `"Sacrificed for the family by not spending on beautifying herself."`[9]
    - **Analysis:** Characterised through constant exhaustion, highlighting how domestic expectations strip her of youth and self-esteem[8][9].
- **Point 2 (Antagonists):** Her family and locals are characterised as hypocritical gatekeepers who exploit her vulnerability[2][4].
    - **Quote 1:** `"He grabbed her by the shoulders and shook her violently."`[17]
    - **Quote 2:** `"Why don't you pay from your salary? She is going to be helping you after all!"`[17]
    - **Analysis:** Her husband is characterised as financially selfish and physically aggressive, treating her as domestic labor rather than a partner[4][17].

---

### **8. Setting & Spatial Atmosphere (Public vs. Private)**

- **Point 1 (Public Setting - Streets & Taxi):** The public urban setting operates as an exclusionary space where locals police her belonging[2][12].
    - **Quote 1:** `"She slammed the door shut, and started walking briskly towards her housing block..."`[3]
    - **Quote 2:** `"You say you're a Singaporean. But you don't know Malay?"`[3]
    - **Analysis:** The public taxi ride and Singapore streets function as hostile settings where her national identity is actively challenged[2][3].
- **Point 2 (Private Setting - HDB Flat & Kitchen):** The domestic home acts as a claustrophobic pressure-cooker of cultural friction and unpaid labor[4][18].
    - **Quote 1:** `"Night had fallen by the time they had reached home."`[5]
    - **Quote 2:** `"He was always this way: detached from the reality of his family responsibilities. People in this country are all the same."`[17]
    - **Analysis:** The HDB apartment is stripped of warmth, functioning as a site of daily conflict, kitchen labor, and marital detachment[4][18].

---

### **9. Narrative Perspective, Focalisation & Internal Monologue**

- **Point 1 (Societal):** Third-person limited focalisation grants direct access to her internal self-doubt and isolation[10][12].
    - **Quote 1:** `"Is it me or them? Maybe I am not easy to get along with. Or have I not adapted to the ways of life here?"`[13]
    - **Quote 2:** `"dreams still like those of an Indian and not a Singaporean?"`[13]
    - **Analysis:** Italicised internal monologue exposes her tragic self-blame, internalising societal rejection as her own failure to adapt[12][13].
- **Point 2 (Domestic):** Internal focalisation highlights the unvoiced agony of her marital exploitation[4][12].
    - **Quote 1:** `"After all, had they not brought her from India for this?"`[4]
    - **Quote 2:** `"Anyway, do I have the luxury of quarrelling with my husband and running home to my mother?"`[17]
    - **Analysis:** Silent internal questions reveal her acute awareness that her arranged marriage was an exploitative transaction[4][17].

---

### **10. Figurative Language (Personification, Metaphor, Simile)**

- **Point 1 (Personification):** Personifies state documents to convey the psychological taunting of unfulfilled citizenship[9][12].
    - **Quote 1:** `"It seemed to smile at her smugly."`[13]
    - **Quote 2:** `"The taxi driver's earlier comments taunted her."`[11]
    - **Analysis:** Personifies the IC and driver's words as mocking tormentors that follow her into her private moments[9].
- **Point 2 (Simile & Metaphor):** Visceral similes capture her degradation within the family household[4][10].
    - **Quote 1:** `"I am sick of this daily routine of thosai, iddili and chutney. Like a beggar."`[5]
    - **Quote 2:** `"she surmised from her perspective: 'People in this country are all the same'"`[4]
    - **Analysis:** Comparing her traditional cooking to food fit for a `"beggar"` metaphorically reduces her cultural heritage to trash[4][5].

---

### **11. Structural Devices (Flashback & Non-Linear Memory)**

- **Point 1 (Flashback to India):** Flashbacks to community life in India highlight her present emotional starvation in Singapore[10].
    - **Quote 1:** `"She recalled with longing the joy she had had with her friends, teasing the bride and groom..."`[13]
    - **Quote 2:** `"She had no such intimate friends here."`[13]
    - **Analysis:** Non-linear memory creates a structural contrast between past collective warmth and present urban isolation[10][13].
- **Point 2 (Flashback to Upbringing):** Internalised memories of her mother's advice reinforce her domestic submissiveness[19].
    - **Quote 1:** `"The voice of her mother in her head telling her to behave in a compliant manner..."`[19]
    - **Quote 2:** `"Had I spoken to my mother like this, she would have thrashed me."`[6]
    - **Analysis:** Flashbacks to her mother's teachings reveal how cultural expectations of filial piety trap her in abusive household dynamics[6][19].

---

### **12. Structural Climax & Ending**

- **Point 1 (Climax of Societal Rejection):** The narrative climax ties paper citizenship directly to her unstemmed grief[8][12].
    - **Quote 1:** `"I am a Singaporean! she reminded herself."`[13]
    - **Quote 2:** `"But she could not stem the tears that coursed down her cheeks."`[13]
    - **Analysis:** The ending juxtaposes her desperate verbal assertion of citizenship against her weeping, proving that official papers cannot cure social exclusion[12][13].
- **Point 2 (Climax of Domestic Trap):** The ending leaves her completely trapped in her daily routine without resolution[4][8].
    - **Quote 1:** `"She had to go to the salon... She needed to coach her son for his Maths test..."`[6][13]
    - **Quote 2:** `"Dismissed her sentimentality for the happier times in India and just get on with her miserable life in Singapore."`[10]
    - **Analysis:** The story ends circularly, forcing her back into immediate domestic duties and leaving her entrapment unresolved[4].
