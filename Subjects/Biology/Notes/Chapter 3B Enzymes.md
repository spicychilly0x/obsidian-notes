
**1. Nature and Function of Enzymes**

- **Definition**: Biological catalysts made primarily of **proteins** that speed up chemical reactions without being changed at the end.
- **Mechanism**: Speed up reactions by **lowering activation energy (**EA​**)**—the minimum energy required to start a reaction.
- **Key Relationship**: Enzymes do **not** affect the change in free energy (ΔG); they only hasten reactions that would eventually occur.
- **Core Characteristics to Memorize**:
    - **Required in minute amounts**: Can be recycled and used over and over.
    - **Highly specific**: Activity is limited to a specific substrate or type of reaction due to unique 3D shapes.
    - **Reversible**: Some can catalyze reactions in both forward and backward directions.
    - **Coenzymes**: Some require organic cofactors to function.

**2. Mode of Action: Two Hypotheses**

- **Lock-and-Key Hypothesis**:
    - The enzyme's **active site** has a specific, rigid shape.
    - The substrate (key) is perfectly **complementary** to the active site (lock).
    - Forms an **enzyme-substrate complex**; products are released because they no longer fit.
- **Induced Fit Hypothesis**:
    - The active site is **physically flexible**, not a perfect initial match.
    - Substrate binding induces a **conformational change** in the enzyme.
    - Allows the active site to fit more snugly around the substrate for catalysis.

**3. Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity**

- **Temperature**:
    - **Low Temperature**: Enzymes are **inactivated**; kinetic energy is too low for frequent collisions.
    - **Increasing Temperature**: Rate doubles for every 10∘C rise as kinetic energy increases, leading to more **effective collisions**.
    - **Optimum Temperature**: The point of maximum activity (often 37∘C to 40∘C in humans).
    - **High Temperature (**>Optimum**)**: Rate drops rapidly due to **denaturation**. Heat disrupts intramolecular bonds (hydrogen/hydrophobic), causing the enzyme to unfold and lose its active site shape. This is **irreversible**.
- **pH**:
    - **Optimum pH**: Specific to each enzyme (e.g., Pepsin at pH 2; Salivary Amylase at pH 7).
    - **Mechanism**: Deviations from optimum change the **ionic charges** on amino acid side chains, disrupting ionic bonds and altering the active site's conformation.
    - **Extreme pH**: Causes permanent denaturation.
- **Concentration (Enzyme or Substrate)**:
    - Increasing concentration increases rate as more collisions occur.
    - **Limiting Factor**: The rate eventually **levels off** (reaches a maximum).
        - If enzyme is constant: All active sites become **saturated** with substrate.
        - If substrate is constant: Substrate becomes the limiting factor.

**4. Enzyme Inhibition**

- **Competitive Inhibition**:
    - **Structure**: Inhibitor resembles the substrate.
    - **Action**: Competes for the **active site**.
    - **Key Relationship**: Can be overcome by **increasing substrate concentration**, which increases the probability of a substrate (rather than an inhibitor) hitting the active site.
- **Non-competitive Inhibition**:
    - **Structure**: No resemblance to substrate.
    - **Action**: Binds to a **region other than the active site**, changing the enzyme’s overall 3D shape (conformation).
    - **Key Relationship**: Cannot be overcome by more substrate; the **maximum rate of reaction is permanently lowered**.

**5. Classification & Applications**

- **Major Classes**:
    - **Hydrolases**: Catalyze hydrolysis (e.g., Lipases, Proteases, Nucleases).
    - **Oxidoreductases**: Oxidation-reduction reactions.
    - **Isomerases**: Rearrange atoms within a molecule.
    - **Ligases**: Join two molecules using ATP.
- **Industrial Examples**:
    - **Protease**: In bio-active detergents to break down protein stains.
    - **Pectinase**: Speeds up fruit juice extraction by breaking down pectin.
    - **Lactase**: Breaks lactose into glucose and galactose for lactose-free milk.
