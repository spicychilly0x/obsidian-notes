# Module 1 — Learn to read an options-flow print

## 1. What exactly are you looking at?

Suppose your options-flow scanner displays this hypothetical trade:

![[Pasted image 20261010000403.png]]

### A. Call or put

- Call: gives its buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy the underlying shares at the strike price.
    
- Put: gives its buyer the right, but not the obligation, to sell the underlying shares at the strike price.
    

A call is not automatically bullish evidence, and a put is not automatically bearish evidence. Someone can sell a call, buy a put as insurance, or combine several contracts into one strategy.

### B. Strike price

The strike is the price at which the option can be exercised.

In our example, the stock is at $100 and the call strike is $110. The call is currently out of the money because the stock price is below the strike.

The stock would need to rise above $110 by expiration for the call to have intrinsic value. But the option can still have value before then because time remains.

### C. Expiration

The contract expires in 90 days.

More time generally gives an option more opportunity to become profitable, although the option's value also depends on volatility, interest rates, dividends and other factors.

For your longer-term investing approach, pay attention to expiration as a clue about the time horizon of the contract — not as proof of how long the trader intends to hold it.

### D. Premium

The premium is the price paid per share of the underlying stock's option.

Here, the calculation is:

\[ \$3.00 \times 100 \times 500 = \$150,000 \]

That means the displayed contracts represent $150,000 in premium, assuming all 500 contracts traded at $3.00.

Crucial distinction: $150,000 in options premium does not mean $150,000 was invested in the stock itself. It also doesn't tell you whether the trade was bullish, bearish, hedged or part of a larger strategy.

## 2. The five questions to ask about every print

Whenever a large transaction appears, train yourself to ask:

1. What was traded? Call or put, strike, expiration and underlying stock.
    
2. How significant was it? Contract volume and premium relative to typical activity in that particular option.
    
3. Where did it execute? Near the bid, ask or midpoint — while remembering that this is only a clue about aggressor direction.
    
4. Was it potentially opening or closing? A single print usually cannot establish this with certainty.
    
5. What else was happening? Earnings, a major announcement, broader market movements, other legs of a strategy or possible hedging.
    

Notice what isn't on that list: “Is this a guaranteed winning trade?” Options flow doesn't give you that answer.
